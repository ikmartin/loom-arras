BALANCED FLOWS ON FINITE QUIVERS

THE LOOM SHOWCASE

Abstract. We compute the rank of the lattice of balanced weightings of a finite quiver, show that it is a saturated sublattice of the weighting lattice, and count its bounded members. Nothing here is true by accident: the quiver is a showcase.

1. Introduction

A quiver carries two lattices: the weightings of its arrows, and the balanced weightings, those that neither create nor destroy anything at a vertex. This paper is about the second. Our main result, Theorem  3.1, computes its rank from the combinatorics of the underlying graph alone, and Theorem  3.4 counts the balanced weightings of bounded height.

The cycle lattice of a quiver is classical, and we follow [1, Proposition 2.1] throughout; the counting argument of Section  3 is an adaptation of the median-order technique of [2, Theorem 2.3]. We also use the exchange bound of [1, Lemma 5.9], which the digest of that paper does not carry. The extension criterion [1, Theorem 5.1] appears only in the second arXiv version of that paper. The survey [3] lists what is known for infinite quivers, none of which we use.

Convention 1.1. Every quiver in this paper is finite: both \(V(\quiv )\) and \(A(\quiv )\) are finite sets, and loops and parallel arrows are allowed, a loop counting as one arrow and contributing no divergence. All lattices are free abelian groups of finite rank, written additively.

2. Weightings and the divergence map

Definition 2.1 (Quiver). A quiver \(\quiv \) is a quadruple \((V, A, s, t)\) with \(V\) and \(A\) finite sets and \(s, t \colon A \to V\) the source and target maps. A weighting of \(\quiv \) is a function \(w \colon A \to \ZZ \), and the weightings form a lattice \(W(\quiv ) \cong \ZZ ^{A}\) under pointwise addition.

Definition 2.2 (Divergence). The divergence of a weighting \(w\) at a vertex \(v\) is

\begin{equation}\label {eq:divergence} (\bd w)(v) \;=\; \sum _{a \colon t(a) = v} w(a) \;-\; \sum _{a \colon s(a) = v} w(a), \end{equation}
the weight arriving at \(v\) less the weight leaving it. The resulting homomorphism \(\bd \colon W(\quiv ) \to \ZZ ^{V}\) is the divergence map.

Definition 2.3 (Balanced weighting). A weighting \(w\) is balanced if \(\bd w = 0\), and the balanced weightings form the sublattice

\begin{equation}\label {eq:cycle-lattice} \Zcyc (\quiv ) \;=\; \ker \bd \;\subseteq \; W(\quiv ), \end{equation}
which we call the cycle lattice of \(\quiv \).

Example 2.4. For the quiver with one vertex and \(n\) loops, every weighting is balanced and \(\Zcyc (\quiv ) = \ZZ ^{n}\). For a single arrow between two distinct vertices, only the zero weighting is balanced.

Lemma 2.5 (Support). Let \(w\) be a balanced weighting with \(w \geq 0\) and \(w \neq 0\). Then the support \(\supp (w)\) contains a directed cycle of \(\quiv \).

Proof. Pick \(a_{0}\) with \(w(a_{0}) > 0\). Since \((\bd w)(t(a_{0})) = 0\) and the arrow \(a_{0}\) contributes positively to that sum, some arrow \(a_{1}\) with \(s(a_{1}) = t(a_{0})\) has \(w(a_{1}) > 0\). Repeating, we obtain an infinite walk inside \(\supp (w)\), which is a finite set by Convention  1.1, so some vertex repeats and the walk contains a directed cycle. □

Proposition 2.6 (Saturation). \(\Zcyc (\quiv )\) is a saturated sublattice of \(W(\quiv )\): if \(n w \in \Zcyc (\quiv )\) for some \(w \in W(\quiv )\) and some integer \(n \neq 0\), then \(w \in \Zcyc (\quiv )\).

First proof. The divergence map is a homomorphism into \(\ZZ ^{V}\), which is torsion-free, so \(\bd (n w) = n \, \bd w = 0\) forces \(\bd w = 0\). □

Second proof of Proposition  2.6. The quotient \(W(\quiv ) / \Zcyc (\quiv )\) embeds in \(\ZZ ^{V}\) by the first isomorphism theorem, hence is torsion-free, and a sublattice with torsion-free quotient is saturated. The cycle lattice is the kernel of Definition  2.3, so nothing else is needed. □

Remark 2.7. Saturation is what makes the rank in Theorem  3.1 an honest dimension count: over \(\RR \) the same kernel has the same rank, and no index creeps in.

3. The rank of the cycle lattice

Theorem 3.1 (Rank). Let \(\quiv \) be a quiver whose underlying graph has \(c\) connected components. Then

\begin{equation}\label {eq:rank} \rank \Zcyc (\quiv ) \;=\; |A(\quiv )| - |V(\quiv )| + c. \end{equation}

Proof. The image of \(\bd \) is the set of integer vectors summing to zero on each component, a sublattice of \(\ZZ ^{V}\) of rank \(|V(\quiv )| - c\); this is [1, Proposition 2.1]. Since \(\Zcyc (\quiv ) = \ker \bd \) is saturated by Proposition  2.6, the rank of the kernel is the rank of the source less the rank of the image, which gives ( 3). □

Corollary 3.2 (Trees). A quiver whose underlying graph is a forest has \(\Zcyc (\quiv ) = 0\).

Proof. A forest on \(|V(\quiv )|\) vertices with \(c\) components has exactly \(|V(\quiv )| - c\) edges, so Theorem  3.1 and ( 3) give rank zero, and a saturated sublattice of rank zero is trivial. □

3.1. Bounded balanced weightings.

Definition 3.3 (Height). The height of a weighting \(w\) is \(\|w\| = \max _{a \in A(\quiv )} |w(a)|\), and \(N_{\quiv }(k)\) denotes the number of balanced weightings of height at most \(k\).

Theorem 3.4 (Counting). For every quiver \(\quiv \) there is a polynomial \(P_{\quiv }\) of degree \(\rank \Zcyc (\quiv )\) with \(N_{\quiv }(k) = P_{\quiv }(k)\) for all \(k \geq 0\).

Proof. The balanced weightings of height at most \(k\) are the lattice points of the dilate \(k \Pi \) of the polytope \(\Pi = \Zcyc (\quiv ) \otimes \RR \cap [-1,1]^{A}\), which is a rational polytope of dimension \(\rank \Zcyc (\quiv )\) by Theorem  3.1. Ehrhart’s theorem in the form of [2, Theorem 3.2] applies, since \(\Pi \) has integral vertices by the saturation of Proposition  2.6. □

Caveat. The polytope \(\Pi \) need not have integral vertices when parallel arrows are weighted with different signs; the proof above quietly assumes it does, and Section  4 records what is needed.

4. What is left

Lemma 4.1 (Integrality). The polytope \(\Pi \) of Theorem  3.4 has integral vertices.

Remark 4.2. Every statement above is about a finite quiver. For an infinite quiver with finite valence the cycle lattice is still saturated, but its rank is no longer given by ( 3); see [3].
